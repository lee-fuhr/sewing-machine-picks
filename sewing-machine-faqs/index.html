Sewing Machine questions answered

Updated June 2026Independently researchedNo paid placement.

Quick answer

Choosing a sewing machine comes down to your skill level and what you plan to sew. For most beginners, a mechanical machine under a few hundred dollars offers the best value, while intermediate quilters or garment-makers often prefer a computerized model with more stitch options. Focus on a machine with a drop-in bobbin, adjustable stitch length and width, and a few essential feet, you can always upgrade later.

What type of sewing machine should a beginner buy?

Start with a mechanical machine, they’re simpler, more durable, and easier to troubleshoot when something goes wrong. A good mechanical machine has a metal internal frame, a basic set of straight and zigzag stitches, and easy-to-use tension dials. You don’t need dozens of decorative stitches to learn the fundamentals. Computerized machines are tempting with their automatic features, but they add complexity and cost. If your budget is tight, a mechanical model will serve you for years and leave money for quality thread, fabric, and classes. The exception is if you know you’ll do a lot of buttonholes or embroidery, then a computerized model might be worth the jump.

How much should I spend on a first sewing machine?

For a first machine, a budget-friendly model in the range of a few hundred dollars or less is plenty to learn on. Avoid the very cheapest machines ( ) because they tend to have plastic gears, poorly calibrated tension, and frustrating feed-dog systems. A few hundred dollars gets you a reliable machine from a known brand that won’t fight you. If you plan to sew heavy fabrics like denim or canvas, expect to spend a bit more, mid-range machines around several hundred dollars often have stronger motors and adjustable presser‑foot pressure. Remember that you’ll also need needles, bobbins, and a few extra feet, so set aside a little extra for those.

Mechanical vs. computerized: which is better?

Mechanical machines give you direct control, you turn dials for stitch length, width, and tension, and you learn exactly what each adjustment does. They’re usually easier to maintain and repair yourself. Many experienced sewers keep a mechanical workhorse for heavy-duty or everyday sewing. Computerized machines offer convenience: automatic buttonholes, needle up/down, speed control, and built-in stitches. They’re great for quilting (especially models with a half‑speed switch) and for people who value precision over simplicity. The trade-off is that electronic boards can fail, and repairs can cost as much as a new machine. Pick computerized if you sew a lot of different projects and want time‑saving features; pick mechanical if you want a simple, long‑lasting machine you can fix yourself.

What stitches do I really need?

The absolute essentials are a straight stitch, a zigzag stitch, and a three‑step zigzag (often called a stretch stitch). That covers seams, finishing raw edges, and sewing knits. A good buttonhole stitch, usually a four‑step or automatic one, is also very useful. Decorative stitches (scallops, flowers, etc.) are fun but rarely necessary. Unless you’re doing garment sewing with lots of topstitching, a machine with 10–20 stitches is plenty. More important than stitch count is the ability to adjust stitch length and width easily, and to move the needle position left and right.

Which accessories should come with a new machine?

Look for a machine that includes at least a general‑purpose foot, a zipper foot, a buttonhole foot, and a button sewing foot. A walking foot (even feed foot) is a huge help for quilting and slippery fabrics, it’s often sold separately, so check if it’s included. You’ll also want a pack of good needles (size 80/12 and 90/14), a few empty bobbins (metal or clear plastic depending on the brand), a lint brush or small brush, and a seam ripper. A hard cover or case is a nice bonus. Avoid machines that come only with the bare minimum of one presser foot and one needle, they’re often made to sell you overpriced accessories later.

How do I choose a reliable brand?

Stick with brands that have been around for decades and have a strong service network: Janome, Brother, Singer, Juki, and Pfaff are the most consistent. Janome is often praised for build quality and smooth stitching. Brother offers excellent value in the lower to mid price range. Singer has a long history but read current owner reviews carefully, some of their budget models use more plastic. Juki makes industrial‑strength home machines (great for heavy sewing), and Pfaff is known for its integrated dual‑feed system. If you buy from a local sewing machine dealer instead of a big‑box store, you usually get a free initial tuning and ongoing support, which can be worth the extra cost.

Frequently asked questions

Can I sew denim on a home sewing machine?

Yes, but you need a machine with a strong motor and a heavy‑duty needle, look for a model that can sew multiple layers of denim without struggling. Many budget mechanical machines can handle two layers of medium‑weight denim, but thick seams (like the hem of jeans) may need a walking foot or a hand‑wheel assist.

Do I need a serger if I have a regular sewing machine?

Not immediately. A serger trims and finishes edges in one pass, which is great for knits and professional‑looking seams, but you can get by with a zigzag stitch or an overcast foot. If you sew a lot of stretch fabrics or want to finish edges quickly, a serger is a nice second machine, not an essential first buy.

What is a walking foot and do I need one?

A walking foot feeds the top layer of fabric at the same rate as the bottom layer, preventing shifting and puckering. It’s very helpful for quilting, sewing slippery or stretchy fabrics, and matching stripes or plaids. If your first project is a quilt or a garment with tricky fabric, a walking foot is worth getting.

How often should I get my sewing machine serviced?

With average use (a few hours per week), have the machine professionally cleaned and oiled once a year. If you sew heavily or use dusty fabrics, twice a year is safer. Between services, always clean out lint under the needle plate and in the bobbin area after each project, that prevents most tension problems.

Should I buy a used sewing machine?

A used vintage machine from a reputable brand (like an all‑metal Singer or Janome) can be a great deal if it’s been serviced. Avoid used computerized machines unless you can test them thoroughly, because electronics can fail and repairs may cost more than the machine. Always try before you buy and ask for a warranty or service history.

What is the difference between a drop‑in bobbin and a front‑loading bobbin?

A drop‑in bobbin sits in a slot under the needle plate, it’s easy to insert and usually has a clear cover so you can see how much thread is left. Front‑loading bobbins require a separate bobbin case that you slide in; they’re older tech but often allow finer tension adjustments. For a beginner, a drop‑in bobbin is much more convenient.

In shortThe best sewing machine for you is the one that matches the projects you actually want to make, not the one with the most stitches or the fanciest display. Start with a reliable mechanical machine from a solid brand, learn to clean and thread it properly, and spend the rest of your budget on good fabric and a few basic feet. Sewing is a skill you build with practice, not with an expensive machine.
